# Learning Process — study notes

## 01 — Way of Thinking
Cybersecurity is too broad to master every technology. Learn transferable principles, research effectively and adapt knowledge. Four key abilities: find, choose, learn and adapt. Reading builds familiarity; hands-on practice builds operational skill. The lesson also introduces abstraction, correspondence and data type completeness as programming principles.

## 02 — Think Outside the Box
Identify assumed constraints rather than blindly accepting them. Consider alternative ways to solve a problem. In security work, creativity must remain within the actual authorization and scope.

## 03 — Occam's Razor
Prefer simpler adequate explanations, while testing evidence. If a computer fails to start, inspect the power cable and switched outlet before replacing components. Understand the mechanism behind a vulnerability rather than memorizing a fixed procedure.

## 04 — Talent
Practice and prior experience develop transferable abilities. A musician's rhythm skills may help when learning a different instrument. Programming experience can likewise help with software security. Natural differences exist, but they do not eliminate the value of training.

## 05 — Way of Learning
Existing mental patterns affect problem solving. Define a clear destination so obstacles do not derail progress. Adapt the approach when necessary and manage frustration.

## 06 — Learning Efficiency
Separate what is known from what is unknown. Combine new information with existing knowledge through practice. Mistakes provide feedback. Prioritize high-value fundamentals using the Pareto heuristic; the 20-hour idea is about initial competence, not guaranteed expertise. The 10,000-hour rule is not a universal requirement.

## 07 — Learning Types
Passive learning includes reading and watching; active learning includes exercises, discussions and experimentation. The course presents a learning pyramid with 10%, 30%, 50% and 75% estimates; these percentages are not reliable universal retention rates. Track small successes, take breaks and notice details.

## 08 — The Brain
The claim that humans use only a small fraction of the brain is false. Learning can strengthen and reorganize neural connections. Conscious and unconscious processing influence behavior. The author's theory of thought is a personal model, not established neuroscience. Libet-style experiments have debated interpretations; learning speed is not a direct measure of intelligence.

## 09 — The Will
Define a desired outcome and deliberately take action. Avoid assuming failure before attempting a task. Practice, revise your approach and maintain realistic expectations.

## 10 — The Goal
Set specific, challenging and achievable goals. Distinguish earning credentials from building genuine competence. Solve exercises independently when possible and use feedback to improve understanding.

## 11 — Decision Making
Choices reflect evidence, priorities, emotions and expected outcomes. Identify missing facts, alternatives and consequences. The trolley thought experiment illustrates assumptions and ethical tradeoffs; it is not a guide to real-world emergency actions. In authorized testing, distinguish actual scope restrictions from imagined technical limitations.

## 12 — Documentation
Identify the audience. Good documentation provides overview, structure and clarity. Technical notes should support reproducibility; customer reports should explain findings, impact and remediation. CherryTree is a note-taking tool; Flameshot captures and annotates screenshots. Avoid ambiguity and unnecessary repetition.

## 13 — Organization
Maintain a roadmap and intermediate checkpoints. Organize references, evidence and tasks before complex work. Consider Scrum, Agile, Kanban, to-do lists and bullet journals. Preparation reduces repeated work.

## 14 — Focus
Focus is the intended subject or goal; attention is what is being processed at a moment; concentration sustains attention. Breaks and alternative activities can help when stuck. Align daily tasks with learning goals.

## 15 — Attention
Attention varies with interests, environment, sleep, goals and emotional state. Observe study habits over a week and adjust session lengths and breaks. Avoid forcing extended study when comprehension declines.

## 16 — Comfort
The course uses the Yerkes–Dodson model to discuss arousal and performance; effects vary with person and task. Comfort, fear, learning and growth zones illustrate gradual exposure to unfamiliar challenges. Mistakes are useful feedback.

## 17 — Obstacles
Fear of failure, rigid beliefs and pressure may hinder progress. Reframe "I cannot do this" as "I cannot do this yet." Manage internal perfectionism and external expectations, take breaks and seek constructive feedback. Talent, skill and passion can reinforce one another.

## 18 — Questioning
Replace overly broad questions with precise, evidence-oriented ones. The course's Relationship-Oriented-Questioning (ROQ) model maps five components: your position, the object, known information, unknown information and other positions. Define the object and at least two components; examine their connections and influence. Rephrase unclear questions as needed and update known information as evidence emerges.

## 19 — Handling Frustration
Frustration can arise from internal expectations or external pressures. In authorized security testing, missing information or resources can cause difficulties. Enumeration is the systematic collection of relevant information about systems and services. Build frustration tolerance through manageable, controlled practice; identify missing information, adjust the approach, and take breaks when needed. Distinguish controllable responses from external circumstances. Frustration is temporary, while lessons learned through practice can persist.

## 20 — Learning Progress
Compare current skills with a dated baseline rather than relying solely on other people's judgments. Maintain two records: a baseline inventory with self-assessed skill ratings from 1 to 10, and a continuous journal of new knowledge and practical experience. Small, consistent learning sessions can support long-term development. The lesson illustrates compounding with 1.00^365 = 1.00 and 1.01^365 ≈ 37.7; this is a mathematical illustration, not a guaranteed rate of human learning.
